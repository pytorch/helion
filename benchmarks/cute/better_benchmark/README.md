# better-benchmark: Inductor Triton vs Helion (CuTe and Triton backends)

[better-benchmark](https://github.com/eellison/better-benchmark) extracts the
fused regions TorchInductor compiles from ~160 real models (torchbench,
HuggingFace, timm, genai; inference and training) as standalone repros under
`repros/canonical/<repro_id>/`. This directory holds hand ports of a sample of
those repros to Helion and the tooling that compares them with the Triton
kernels Inductor generates for the same repros.

The better-benchmark checkout is found through `BETTER_BENCHMARK_ROOT`
(default: `../better-benchmark` next to this repository). `selection.json`
pins better-benchmark commit `f65c998a8bcee982ccbf67b52fd9788bbae7b1bb` (its
`better_benchmark_commit`): the ports read the repros' inputs by position,
and the scripts warn when the checkout is at another commit. The first 51
ports were made against `824c970f25f052de6cf66a4c634df246af00bb1e`, the other
99 against the pinned commit; `repros/` and the model accounting are the same
at both.

## Layout

- `kernels/<repro_id>.py`: one Helion port per repro (see `PORTING.md`).
- `selection.json`: the 150 measured (repro, shape) points and why each was
  picked: the study's 48 sampled points, three extra ported repros
  (`"reason": "extra"`), then the next 99 repros by model share
  (`"next model share"`), generated by
  `select_kernels.py` from a better-benchmark all-shapes sweep
  (`scripts/bench_parallel.py repros/canonical --all-shapes --output ...`) and
  better-benchmark's B200 model accounting. Without `--shape`, the scripts
  use a repro's point from it (a repro outside it: its first shape point).
- `common.py`: loading repros, inputs, ports and pinned configs; numerics checks.
- `inductor_code.py`: dump the Triton code Inductor generates for a repro.
- `check_port.py`: check a port on both Helion backends (optionally with random
  configs) against eager PyTorch and the anchored numerics gate, a loosened
  variant of better-benchmark's (it also accepts a one-ulp difference per
  bf16 or fp16 element, and compares the outputs that depend on random
  numbers with Inductor's). Like better-benchmark's, its reference is eager
  PyTorch on float64 copies of the inputs with the graph's own casts kept,
  so for a graph that converts to fp32 after its loads it is fp32 math
  (`common.AnchoredGate`). Integer and boolean outputs must equal eager's,
  with one exception that `autotune.py` and `bench.py` share
  (`common.check_outputs`): an output that Inductor's own default compile
  does not reproduce either must equal that compile's output instead,
  element for element. Eager returns the tied values of a `topk` in an
  implementation-defined order and Inductor's decomposition in index order,
  so the sort permutation that pointwise_59acdb823e92 returns differs
  between the two in 40 of 4000 elements, and a port of Inductor's kernels
  can only reproduce Inductor's. `check_port.py` prints each output it
  judged that way ("exact output vs Inductor") and the autotune and bench
  records list it under `exact_vs_inductor`, with the number of elements in
  which the implementation's and Inductor's outputs differ from eager's.
  An output that Inductor reproduces is compared with eager only, and
  `autotune.py` compiles the repro with Inductor only for a port with an
  integer or boolean output that differs from eager's. Every call that
  `check_port.py`, the gate and `bench.py` judge starts from poisoned
  memory, and an output that still holds the poison fails its
  implementation (Unwritten outputs, below). `--configs-dir RUN/configs`
  also checks each port pinned to the pick of a campaign.
- `autotune.py`: cold full autotune of one port on one backend; records each
  kernel's winning `@helion.kernel(...)` decorator. On the CuTe backend a
  kernel that the port runs behind earlier kernels is tuned behind them (How
  a port is tuned, below).
- `bench.py`: time Inductor (default, coordinate descent, and the options of
  `mode="max-autotune"`; the comparison is against the fastest), the repro's
  hand-written Triton oracle, both Helion backends and a memcopy
  speed-of-light proxy, interleaved in one process, and check each on a
  CUDA graph replay, the way it is timed.
- `campaign.py`: run autotune and bench jobs for a selection across GPUs;
  a job during which another process computed on its GPU runs again
  (`--retry-contaminated`). `--configs-from` benches another run's picks,
  `--seeds-from` searches from another run's random seeds, `--bench-repeats`
  benches every point in several fresh processes (Baseline, below),
  `--regime` names one tuning regime for both backends (`alone` tunes every
  kernel alone as the runs before the port regime did), and every search's
  per-config log is kept under the run's `logs/search`.
- `plan_run.py`: plan a campaign from earlier runs' records: every CuTe
  autotune, the Triton autotunes whose earlier records cannot be reused
  (contaminated, unsampled, tuned in another regime than the run's, or no
  longer generating the code they were tuned as), every bench, and the
  GPU-hours they should take.
- `report.py`: summarize a campaign: geomean, time-weighted and
  model-weighted speedups over Inductor with and without the T5 outlier,
  per-family rows, %SOL, Helion-Triton, and each kernel next to a
  `--baseline` run, with how many of the run's autotunes searched from that
  run's seeds; contaminated and unsampled records are listed and left out
  of the aggregates, and a section says how many kernels were tuned behind
  the earlier kernels of their port.
- `audit_activity.py`: list a run's records whose activity samples do not
  cover their jobs (no samples, too many failed ones, a long stretch without
  one, or a sampling thread that died), with the numbers it went by.
- `ncu_profile.py`: profile one implementation of a repro under Nsight
  Compute (per-kernel duration, DRAM and L2 traffic, throughput, occupancy,
  launch geometry).

## Timers

`bench.py` records two latencies per implementation, both cold-L2 (and a
third on request, below):

- `bb_us`: better-benchmark's own methodology (single-call CUDA graph replay,
  `do_bench` minimum); includes one graph launch.
- `cold_us`: the regime Helion's autotuner searches in (`interleaved_bench`:
  L2 flushed before every call inside a batched CUDA graph, flush time
  subtracted); free of launch-latency quantization, so it separates small
  kernels better. A Triton search also picks its final config in this regime.
  A CuTe search picks it by the kernels' durations in the CUDA activity trace
  (`CuteBackend.default_final_selection_regime`, also flushed per call), which
  also time the candidates of a search whose best initial config reads under
  64 us (`autotune_short_kernel_below_us`);
  `HELION_AUTOTUNE_FINAL_L2_FLUSH=1` makes it select in this one.

The rounds alternate the order of the implementations, and a reading depends
on what ran right before it (by up to a microsecond for a small kernel), so
each timing is the mean of its forward-order and reverse-order values over
an even number of rounds (6 by default). Records made before that took the
median of 5 cold rounds, a forward-order reading, and the minimum over all
bb rounds: `report.py --rebalance` recomputes their timings from the
recorded rounds.

What ran right before a batch matters by implementation, and the two orders
give an implementation its two listing neighbours as predecessors and no
other, at two mirror places of the round. Measured on B200s with a test
build of `bench.py` that ran, after every round, one more in the next order
of a design in which each implementation takes every place once and runs
right behind every other one once (a Williams design), on the same inputs,
with the study's six implementations (the three Inductor compiles, the
oracle, `helion_cute`, the memcopy) in fresh processes of ten ports:

- no place of a round reads high or low for every implementation: fitted
  with each implementation's own time and what its predecessor adds, the
  places differ by at most 0.15 us a call (five ports of 7 to 17 us, 21
  processes);
- the batch right behind `helion_cute`'s read higher on three of the seven
  ports under 33 us, by 0.6 us a call on average over the implementations
  behind it (pointwise_03afed5ec8e9, var_mean_c3ea8f7ce5c6 and
  pointwise_04d85912d998: 0.61, 0.67 and 0.62 us in `cold_us`, 0.75, 0.66
  and 0.45 us in `rot_us`; up to 1.0 us for one implementation), and by
  less than 0.15 us in `cold_us` and 0.3 us in `rot_us` on the other four.
  Why those three ports is open: all seven picks load some input with an
  `l2_last` or `l1_l2_last` hint;
- `helion_cute`'s own reading follows its place in the round, not what ran
  before it. Against its mean over the design's six rounds it read, on
  those three ports, 0.1 to 0.4 us higher in the second place (behind the
  oracle in the design, behind the memcopy in the listing's reverse
  rounds: the same within 0.2 us), 0.1 to 0.5 us lower in the fifth
  (behind `inductor_max` in the design, behind the oracle in the listing's
  forward rounds: the same within 0.15 us) and 0.4 us lower in the last,
  behind the memcopy; on pointwise_ef561a2def29 the other way round (0.3
  to 0.5 us lower second, 0.5 us higher fifth). The listing's two places
  for it mirror each other, and their mean is within 0.05 us of the mean
  of the six on all four ports;
- single rounds of other pairs differ by up to 1 us; a pair has one round
  of the design, which does not tell its predecessor from its place;
- `bb_us` shows none of it: every implementation is timed alone there.

In the listing order of `common.ALL_IMPLS`, and in the study's (the same
without `helion_triton`), what runs right behind a Helion port is the
memcopy, the oracle or the other port, never an Inductor compile. In the
study's the port itself runs fifth, behind the oracle, and second, behind
the memcopy, and its timing comes out as in the balanced rounds of the same
process, where it takes every place and runs behind every implementation
once: +0.08 +- 0.13% (the listing's rounds over the balanced ones, 32
processes of the ten ports, 7 to 130 us; by port from -0.5% to +0.5%, +0.3
and -0.5% on the first two ports above), so neither those places nor those
predecessors favour it on average. The fastest Inductor compile over it
comes out the same as well: -0.33 +- 0.17% (by port from -1.3% to +0.3%). If
anything the listing's rounds read the ratio lower, so the rounds stay as
they are, and a ratio that is to compare with the study's keeps that
listing: an Inductor compile listed next to a Helion port, or a set without
the oracle between them, puts that compile right behind the port in half its
rounds. Without the oracle and the memcopy, `inductor_max` read 1.0 and 0.4
us higher in its reverse rounds on the first two ports above and the ratio
2% higher (two processes each). A port listed last is followed by the first
implementation of the order, at the start of the next sample; where the
round times batches (ports whose fastest implementation takes under 64 us a
call) the flush-only graph runs between them: `inductor` read 0.08 and 0.25
us higher in those rounds on the same two ports (two processes each), where
it read 0.9 and 1.1 us higher right behind the port, and the flush-only
graph itself took the same time behind the port's batch as behind the
others'. A longer port is timed replay by replay, each behind a flush of its
own, so the first implementation follows the port there as any neighbour in
the order does (what a port leaves was measured on batched ports only, and
seen on three of 7 to 17 us). `bench.py` records what ran right behind each
Helion port in the rounds of each order (`behind_helion`), and warns, with
the compiles named in the record (`carryover_risk`), when a listing puts an
Inductor compile there or first in an order that the port ends. An aggregate
of several processes keeps each process's followers
(`behind_helion_per_process`) and every such compile, and `report.py` lists
the benches that have one ("Inductor compiles behind a Helion port").

Several Helion ports listed side by side (variants of one port) are not
interchangeable slots either. One record loaded four times, in the three
Inductor slots and its own (three ports of 7 to 12 us, 15 processes), read
1.3 +- 0.3% lower in its own slot than in the other three with these
rounds, 0.9 +- 0.3% lower with balanced ones and the same with fresh timed
inputs before every round on top, up to 4% apart across the four slots of
one port, the same way in 5 of 5 processes. The default Inductor compile
timed in the same four slots did not (-0.3 +- 0.3% with either rounds). So
neither the round order nor where the inputs lie decides it, and what does
is open (`bb_us`, which no predecessor moves, has slot differences too, up
to 5.6%). Compare variants over several processes, with each variant in
every slot equally often.

`bench.py` also records `kernel_us`, the device time of the kernels one
replay launches, from a profile of back-to-back replays (L2-warm), with each
kernel's share (`kernels`). A kernel's time there is its step: the end of
its profiler span minus the later of the span's start and the latest end
before it. For kernels that run one after the other that is the span. A
kernel launched as a programmatic dependent launch (a CuTe pick with
`cute_pdl`) starts its span while its predecessor still runs, so the spans
overlap: the first BatchNorm kernel of pointwise_06377502b8f5 has a span of
66 us and a step of 16 us, and the spans of that port's five kernels add up
to 206 us of a 162 us replay. Records keep the spans as each kernel's
`span_us` and the implementation's `kernel_span_us` (what `us` and
`kernel_us` were before), and say whether the two totals differ
(`kernel_spans_overlap`); `report.py` lists those implementations.

`bench.py --rotate-inputs N` adds a third, opt-in timing, `rot_us`, as a
cross-check of `cold_us` on small kernels. `cold_us` replays every call on
the same input tensors, and lines loaded with an `L2::evict_last` hint stay
in L2 through the 256 MB flush (Triton's `eviction_policy="evict_last"`,
which Inductor emits on the operands it expects to read again; CuTe's
`l2_last` load policies, which a search can pick), so an implementation that
hints its loads reads part of its inputs warm and the comparison depends on
who hints what. For `rot_us` every implementation gets N private copies of
the inputs (another implementation's hinted lines cannot serve its reads)
and is timed as a graph of N x [flush; call on the next copy] minus the N
flushes, in the same order-balanced rounds. On a B200 with N = 8, a CuTe
config of var_mean_var_mean_0dd15b92dc70 reads 11.7 us in `cold_us` with
`l2_last` on its loads and 14.1 us without, and 14.6 and 14.4 us in `rot_us`;
kernels of 9 to 24 us without such hints and the memcopy read within 0.5 us
of their `cold_us`. Its limits:

- It is not a proof of coldness. Hinted lines are not kept forever, nothing
  shows that N other sets displace all of them, and readings rise with N
  without a plateau that would mark a cold time, a hinted kernel's most: over
  its `cold_us`, Inductor on that port reads 1.1, 1.5 and 1.9 us more at
  N = 4, 8 and 16 and the CuTe config without hints -0.1, 0.4 and 0.5 us, so
  the ratio moves too (1.08, 1.09, 1.11). Compare readings taken with one N;
  8 is the count the study used.
- It leans against hinted kernels as `cold_us` leans towards them. A kernel
  that hints its loads reads worse than the same kernel without the hints:
  Inductor on mean_mean_7291b09200a4 reads 9.4 us in `cold_us` and 10.8 us in
  `rot_us`, and 9.8-10.2 us by either when compiled with its hints stripped
  (which coordinate descent then tunes differently). Part of that is time
  outside the kernels: `rot_us` is timed between CUDA events like `cold_us`,
  and under the same rotation Inductor on 0dd15 reads 15.5 us where the span
  of its kernels in the CUDA activity trace is 14.3 us (three other
  readings: 0.0 to 0.3 us over the span). Hinted lines also cost what is
  timed beside them a few tenths of a microsecond (the memcopy of that 0dd15
  port reads 10.8 us beside the config with `l2_last`, 10.4 us beside the
  one without).
- The two timers bracket a ratio only when one side hints. A CuTe config of
  7291 without hints (9.0 us by both timers) is 1.05x faster than Inductor
  by `cold_us` and 1.21x by `rot_us`, either side of the 1.09-1.14x it is
  against Inductor compiled without hints. With `l2_last` on the same
  config's loads (7.9 and 9.3 us) the timers agree, at 1.21x and 1.19x, and
  both are high (1.06-1.14x against that compile): in `cold_us` CuTe's lines
  are worth more warm than Inductor's, in `rot_us` Inductor's kernel loses
  more to its hints than CuTe's. Inductor always hints, so agreement between
  the timers settles nothing where the CuTe config hints too: look for
  `l2_last` in the `load_eviction_policies` of the record's `decorators`
  before reading a ratio.
- A reading also moves with which tensors a kernel replays, with nothing
  rotating, so not all of `rot_us - cold_us` is lines that rotation
  displaced. With a single set in place of the N, default Inductor on 7291
  read 1.1 to 1.7 us above its `cold_us` on a fresh set and 0.3 us above on
  the set `cold_us` had replayed, on one GPU of four (four processes and
  two); the other implementations and GPUs stayed within 0.6 us.
- Compare ratios within a timer, not `rot_us` with `cold_us`: the rounds of
  the two run at different times, and a power-capped GPU's clock moves with
  the load (the Inductor and CuTe kernels of max_amax_sum_bbcd2529e855 read
  about 5% higher in `rot_us`, its memcopy the same).
- The Inductor side of a ratio is one sample per bench, in either timer. A
  campaign's bench has a cache directory of its own, so coordinate descent
  tunes again in each, and its pick moves a reading by more than the timers
  differ: over eight benches of the same 7291 configs Inductor's tuned
  compile read 9.3 to 11.6 us in `cold_us` and 10.2 to 13.4 us in `rot_us`
  (its default compile 10.9 to 11.1 us and 13.1 to 13.3 us, the CuTe port
  7.7 to 7.9 us and 9.0 to 9.1 us). `campaign.py --bench-repeats` takes
  several samples (Baseline, below).
- It covers small ports only, and a record's `rotate_inputs` field says why
  a port was left out: inputs that total more than twice the device's L2
  (`--rotate-max-l2`; the L2 is 126.5 MiB on a B200, and none of the seven
  ports measured with 127 MB of inputs or more showed an effect of hints); a
  fastest implementation of 64 us or more, which `cold_us` times as single
  replays, so that a batch would not be its cross-check; or N sets for every
  implementation that do not fit a quarter of the free device memory. By
  input size and run time, 24 of the first 51 selected points take it while the
  memcopy is among the timed implementations, as it is by default: it is the
  fastest on the larger ports (`rotate_inputs` names the fastest), and
  without it five of the 24, whose fastest port takes 74 to 132 us, are
  left out.

To add it to the picks of a finished run `RUN` without autotuning again or
touching its records, bench them into a run directory of their own, which
`report.py` reports like any run, with the `rot_us` speedup beside the
`cold_us` one:

```bash
S=benchmarks/cute/better_benchmark
scripts/gpu_board.py run --gpus 0,1 --purpose "better-benchmark RUN-rot" -- \
    python $S/campaign.py --selection $S/selection.json --run-dir RUN-rot \
    --configs-from RUN/configs --phases bench --rotate-inputs 8 --gpus 0,1
python $S/report.py RUN-rot
```

A bench that has finished does not run again for the option (in `RUN`
itself, or in `RUN-rot` with another N): the campaign names such records in
a `rotate_inputs_not_added` event, and `--rebench` runs them again. Started
again, the campaign makes `RUN-rot`'s copies those of the picks it is given
then: it copies the picks that `RUN` has autotuned again since (or another
run's, given that run's `configs`) and removes its copies of picks that are
not there. Its benches time the Helion ports of `--backends` only, so a
bench pins no record but the picks it is given, whatever else the run
directory holds. A point is benched again when its bench record was timed on
other picks than `RUN-rot` then holds; the bench record names the kernels
each port was pinned to, so this does not depend on file dates or on which
start of the campaign made the copy. `--configs-from` replaces and removes
only the copies it made (`RUN-rot/configs_copied.json` lists them): it
stops, before changing anything, on a run directory with autotune records
of its own that are not among the picks it is given, so it cannot replace
the records of the run it is pointed at by mistake, and on a directory that
holds none of the selected picks (`RUN` for `RUN/configs`).

Throughput is reported against better-benchmark's effective byte count
(`byte_accounting.count_bytes_effective`: unique inputs read plus outputs
written).

## How a port is tuned

`autotune.py` runs the port once; every kernel searches at its first call,
in launch order, on the tensors the port hands it. A search times a
candidate's own run time after an L2 flush (the Timers above). That is how
the bench runs a port's first kernel: `cold_us` flushes once, before the
port. A later kernel runs behind the kernels before it, which have just
written or read its operands: they are in L2, wholly when they fit (126.5 MiB
on a B200) and their tail when they do not, and the lines the flush dirtied
are partly gone. A kernel's tiles and layouts can rank in opposite orders in
the two states. On pointwise_ef561a2def29 (BatchNorm+ReLU, then a padded 3x3
max pool over the 12.8 MB it wrote), 19 configs of the pool timed alone and
timed in the port rank in opposite orders (rank correlation -0.81), and the
config that reads fastest alone is 10% behind the port's fastest. On
sum_2ba0992ca00b (column sums over the 201 MB a GELU backward wrote) the
correlation over 15 configs is -0.58 and the fastest alone is 6% behind.

So a kernel that runs behind earlier kernel calls of its port is tuned behind
them (`--regime port`, the CuTe backend's default). The search's benchmark
worker runs those calls, each at the config it was just tuned to and on the
port's own tensors, between the L2 flush and every timed call of a
candidate, and hands the candidate what they produced
(`common.kernels_in_sequence`, through the autotuner's internal
`helion.autotuner.benchmark_prelude`). The reading stays the candidate's own
time: an event pair starts after the earlier kernels have run, and the CUDA
activity trace reads only the kernels behind a marker launched after them.
Every earlier call is replayed, whether or not its output feeds the kernel,
because what it read and wrote is in L2 too. The port's first kernel has
nothing before it and is timed alone.

Measured on a B200 with the CuTe backend (cold searches of the later kernels
with the campaign's seeds, the first kernels pinned; each port then benched
in 5 fresh processes with the old and the new picks pinned in the same
process; `cold_us` against the fastest Inductor compile, median [range]; the
gain is the old picks' time over the new picks', paired per process):

```text
point                       later kernels                           picks tuned alone      tuned in the port      gain
pointwise_ef561a2def29      max_pool_padded                         1.049 [1.039..1.074]   1.138 [1.124..1.156]   1.082 [1.062..1.101]
sum_2ba0992ca00b            column_partial_sums, sum_partials       0.985 [0.984..1.000]   1.025 [1.024..1.043]   1.040 [1.040..1.044]
pointwise_b0a24575ebc9      max_pool                                1.024 [1.017..1.034]   1.028 [1.017..1.034]   1.000 [1.000..1.005]
sum_sum_sum_907cbb3d9f19    sum_gradient_partials, select_scatter   1.295 [1.179..1.309]   1.287 [1.165..1.307]   0.993 [0.986..1.000]
```

The first port gains 8 to 11%. A bench times every implementation in a slot
of its own (its own copies of the inputs, its own neighbours in the round
order), and the slots are not alike for a port this short: its new picks,
pinned a second time in the same five processes, read 10.53 us where the
first slot read 10.81 us (gain 1.112 [1.098..1.138] against 1.082). Its
`rot_us` gains 5.0% [4.0..5.6]. The third port's max pool reads 354 MB, far
more than L2 holds, and takes 68 us: the two ways of timing pick alike
there. The fourth port is a control (LayerNorm backward, far above Inductor
either way), and it loses 0.7%: its new picks read 79.9 us in some rounds
and 81.9 us in others, one step of the bench's event clock, where the picks
tuned alone read 79.8 us in every round (the same new picks in a second
slot: 0.992 [0.987..1.000]). The loss is not explained further.

A second control, amax_sum_sum_c94a0970300f, was measured on an earlier
revision of the regime, whose final selections did not yet start with the
sweep of pinned L2 lines (the autotuner's `benchmark_prelude` says why they
do): level (0.998 [0.996..0.999]). That revision's picks for the control
above lost 2.5% in the processes of the table (0.975 [0.975..0.975]; 1.4%
and 3.6% in two more benches). Its final selection picked the scatter
kernel without evict-last load hints, where the searches alone, and now the
port regime's, pick them. That is one search each: the difference may be
the sweep, which keeps one finalist's hinted lines from serving the next
finalist's window, or the spread between searches.

The readings a search takes behind the earlier kernels rank a kernel's
configs as the port does, where the readings alone rank them backwards:
rank correlation +0.83 against -0.81 over 19 configs of the max pool of
pointwise_ef561a2def29 (the config that reads fastest behind costs the port
1.9%, the one fastest alone 10.0%; the eight configs of the winning tile
shape are within 0.2 us of each other in the port), and +0.78 against -0.58
over 15 configs of the column sums of sum_2ba0992ca00b (0.7% and 6.2%). A
later kernel that reads only inputs no earlier kernel touched reads alike
either way: 109.2 us [108.3..111.2] alone and 110.3 us [109.0..111.5] behind
the three calls before it, six windows each (softmax_dropout_bwd of
sum_sum_sum_d2c97d17f3dc, over 370 MB).

What the record says, and what follows from it:

- `regime` is the record's (`port`, or `alone`: Triton's default, and
  either backend's under `--regime alone`), and `regimes` names, per kernel,
  how its search was timed (`regime`), why (`reason`), the kernels launched
  before it (`behind`) and, from the search's own final selection, how many
  earlier calls each timed call ran behind (`prelude_steps`). Every search
  that has a final selection is held to the kernel call it ran in (the
  search's metrics name it, `port_call`): one that ran another number of
  earlier calls than its call was given fails the record. A bench record
  names the regime of the autotune record it pinned (`autotune_regime`,
  `autotune_regimes`), and `report.py` counts each backend's kernels of each
  kind and lists the kernels that a record in the port regime tuned alone
  (Tuning regime).
- Triton ports keep the old regime (`alone`) unless `--regime port` is
  given: a Triton search reads every candidate between two CUDA events,
  and no Triton port was measured in the port regime (behind earlier
  kernels such a reading is coarse for a short kernel: the 6 us max pool
  above read 18 to 19 us in 2 us steps, rank correlation -0.12 with the
  port). A comparison of the two backends therefore tunes them in
  different regimes; `--regime` names one for both.
- A record made before the regime existed names none and counts as `alone`:
  every one of its kernels was timed alone. It still benches. `plan_run.py`
  reuses a Triton record of a port with several kernels only for a run
  whose Triton autotunes run in the record's regime (by default they do:
  `alone`), and `campaign.py --regime alone` repeats the old regime on
  both backends for a comparison with an earlier run. Give `plan_run.py`
  and the campaign of its run the same `--regime`: the plan names the
  regime it reused Triton records for, and `campaign.py` refuses to run in
  another.
- A kernel is tuned alone, with the reason in the record, when it is the
  port's first call, or when one of its arguments views an earlier kernel's
  output as another dtype (the sequence follows tensors by their storage,
  not their bits). So is every kernel after that one: the call that read
  the view cannot be replayed on what the earlier kernels make. A kernel
  that `forward` reaches other than through its module-level name is not
  followed at all: the port regime fails such a record (the kernels after
  it would be tuned behind preludes without its calls); under `--regime
  alone` it is tuned as every kernel is and has no entry in `regimes`.
- A kernel that the port calls several times searches at its first call,
  behind the calls before that one; its later calls are replayed for the
  kernels after them.
- Only kernel calls are replayed. A tensor that host code makes between two
  kernels (the `torch.sort` of pointwise_59acdb823e92) is a constant of the
  later kernels' searches: they read it flushed, as they would tuned alone,
  although the port has just written it, and the record does not tell such
  an operand from a port input. A buffer the port allocates and an earlier
  kernel fills is filled again before every timed call.
- The inputs are the search's for its whole length: a kernel that writes
  one in place (BatchNorm's running statistics) leaves it written for the
  next timed call, as the bench's replays of the port do.
- The port regime needs the searches' default timers: a job environment
  that sets `HELION_BENCHMARK_CUDAGRAPH=1`, or an L2 regime that does not
  flush before every call (`HELION_AUTOTUNE_L2_FLUSH`,
  `HELION_AUTOTUNE_FINAL_L2_FLUSH`, `HELION_AUTOTUNE_TARGET_REGIME`), fails
  the autotune of a port with several kernels with the autotuner's error.
  `--regime alone` takes them as before: it gives no search a prelude, so
  every search is made and timed as it was before the regime existed (two
  searches from one seed still differ by their readings).
- The earlier kernels run before every timed call, so a search's wall time
  grows by their run time per timed call. The searches above took about as
  long as earlier searches of the same kernels alone: 462 s against 399 s
  for the max pool of pointwise_ef561a2def29 (440 configs against 346), 616
  s against 696 s for the two kernels of sum_2ba0992ca00b, 575 s against
  542 s for the two of sum_sum_sum_907cbb3d9f19, 435 s against 570 s for
  the max pool of pointwise_b0a24575ebc9.

## Baseline: several bench processes

One bench process is one sample of a point, of the Inductor side most of
all, and two things move a sample of a small kernel by more than the 1% a
comparison near parity is about:

- Inductor's pick. A bench has a cache directory of its own, so coordinate
  descent tunes `inductor_cd` and `inductor_max` (the options of
  `mode="max-autotune"`, which include it) again in every process, and it
  does not end on the same config every time. Over eight processes of
  var_mean_c3ea8f7ce5c6 `inductor_cd` ended on `XBLOCK=1` with one warp,
  `XBLOCK=1` with two and `XBLOCK=2` with one, and read 10.5 to 11.1 us; the
  mix-order reductions' compiles pick another split size from process to
  process (`RSPLIT_SIZE` 8, 16, 32 or 64), which moved those points'
  baselines by 8 to 21%. A bench record names the config that each Triton
  kernel of every Inductor compile (`inductor`, `inductor_cd`,
  `inductor_max`) ended on (`inductor_configs`: block sizes, warps, stages,
  a mix-order kernel's split size, registers), read from the kernels the
  compiled code launches, and `report.py` lists them.
- Placement. A small kernel's reading depends on where its buffers lie in
  device memory. One CuTe config of that point read 11.05 to 12.07 us on six
  input sets of the same values in one process, timed in rotation, and
  Inductor's kernel 11.26 to 11.91 us on its six. A process allocates in the
  same order every time, so the same placement came back in every process
  and read as an effect of an implementation's place in `--impls`: with the
  buffers where the allocation order puts them, the CuTe port read 10.65 us
  listed fourth (ratio to Inductor 1.023 [1.023..1.036], three processes)
  and 11.42 us listed second (0.954 [0.952..0.968]). What ran before a
  kernel was not the cause there: the batched graph of each implementation
  took the same time after every other implementation's (within 0.1 us a
  call, with the picks of that time; with a later campaign's the batch
  right behind the port's read up to 1 us higher on this port, see Timers).
  `bench.py` therefore draws the placement: before each implementation's
  timed inputs and before the timers it releases the allocator's cached
  blocks and holds a block of a random number of 2 MiB pages (1 to 128),
  so the buffers come from the driver somewhere else in every process
  (`--placement-seed` repeats a record's draw, `--fixed-placement` leaves
  the buffers where the allocation order puts them, as in the records made
  before). With it the same two orders read 0.988 [0.953..1.005] and 0.969
  [0.961..1.010] over four processes each. One process still times one
  placement of each implementation.

  The draw removes the bias of the listing order and puts spread in its
  place. The driver lays memory out in chunks of 32 MiB, so the 128
  spacer sizes put a buffer of 32 MiB or more at one of 9 places, and a
  smaller one at a few more (15 for 3 MB). `placement` in the record
  names what the draw came to: each spacer's pages and address, and the
  addresses of the buffers each implementation's timed call reads. Over
  those eight processes the ratio of this 10.7 us point had a standard
  deviation of 2.4% from process to process, where it was 0.8% with the
  buffers left in place (around a value that the listing order had moved
  by 7%). The median of three such processes is therefore known to about
  1.7%, and of nine to about 1%: a point this small needs more repeats
  than three before its ratio settles a 1% question, and the range of
  per-process ratios that the report prints says how far it is from
  that. `rot_us` (`--rotate-inputs 8`) reads each implementation on eight
  private input sets, which lie at eight addresses, and is the
  cross-check for these points.

`campaign.py --bench-repeats N` takes N samples of both: it benches every
point in N fresh processes, each with its own Inductor cache and placement
(`bench/<point>.r1.json` to `.rN.json`, each retried when contaminated like
any bench), and writes `bench/<point>.json` as their aggregate
(`common.aggregate_bench_records`): every implementation's `cold_us`,
`bb_us`, `rot_us` and `kernel_us` are the medians over the processes, and
`per_process` keeps each process's values and Inductor picks. A process
whose record is contaminated is left out; an implementation that failed, or
failed the numerics gate, in one process is failed in the aggregate.
`report.py` and its `--goal` file read the aggregate, by this rule:

- in each process, Inductor is the fastest of its compiles in that process
  (default, coordinate descent and the options of `mode="max-autotune"`:
  `common.INDUCTOR_IMPLS`), and the ratio is that time over the port's time
  in the same process (the timings one process interleaved on one GPU);
- the point's ratio is the median of the per-process ratios.

A port, an oracle or the memory copy that failed in one process has no
timing and no ratio for the point. An Inductor compile that failed in one
process, or failed the gate there, is missing from that process's baseline
alone, as it would be from a single bench: the fastest of the other
compiles stands for Inductor in that process, and the fastest of them all
in the others (the report lists such points under "Inductor compiles
missing from a process"). A process without any compile leaves the point
without a ratio.

The fastest of several compiles is a minimum of noisy readings, and it reads
low where the compiles are close: in the processes of one campaign whose
three compiles ended on the same kernel configs, it read 1.5% under the mean
of the three for ports under 20 us (19 of 48 processes) and 0.3% for larger
ones. The ratio above stays the one the goal is stated in. Beside it every
bench record holds a de-biased baseline for `cold_us` and `rot_us`
(`fastest_inductor`, `common.fastest_inductor`: the compiles that ended on
the same code pooled round by round, and the fastest distinct one chosen on
other rounds than it is timed on; an aggregate holds each process's,
`fastest_inductor_per_process`; `bb_us`, a minimum over every replay by
design, has none). Timed against the same compile in a fourth slot of the
process, the fastest of three slots read 0.60% low and the three pooled
0.19% high (+- 0.34, 15 processes of three ports of 7 to 12 us); choosing on
other rounds takes off only what the rounds' noise adds (under a third of it
there), since a compile's luck in a process holds through its rounds, so
distinct compiles that are equally fast keep most of the bias. The report
shows the port's speedup over the de-biased baseline in columns of their own
(`cute/ind de-biased`, and `cute/ind rot de-biased` beside the `rot_us`
speedup), with the count of kernels whose two speedups lie more than 0.5%
apart: 9 of the 62 points of that campaign in `cold_us` (7 of its 16 under
20 us), by up to 2.3%, all of them upwards, and 4 of 39 in `rot_us`.
`report.py` takes the figure from the rounds of records made before it was
stored, and for an aggregate made before from its processes' records while
they are the ones it was made of (their digests), so such runs show it too.
A record with a compile that does not name its kernels' configs has none
(`inductor_configs`: missing in a record made before `bench.py` noted them,
empty for a compile that launches no Triton kernel): which compiles are one
code is then unknown, and without the pooling it would keep most of the bias
under this name. Nor has one whose timings are not the order-balanced ones,
until `report.py --rebalance` recomputes them: its `cold_us` is another
statistic of the rounds than the figure. Compiles are one code when their
kernels' entries agree in all but who found the config, the binary's hash
in Triton's cache included (`cache_hash`, the same in every process for the
same code); a record made before its entries named the hash is compared by
the rest alone.

The ratio of the medians is not that number, and is not used: the fastest
compile is another one from process to process, so the median of each
compile's times describes a baseline that no process timed, and a slow
draw of one compile would hide behind the other's median. The report shows
each point's range of per-process ratios beside the median. Tools that read
`bench/*.json` themselves see the per-process records beside the
aggregates (`common.is_repeat_record` tells them apart). An aggregate's
`wall_s` and activity samples are those of its processes added up, and its
samples cover it when each process's cover that process
(`unknown_allowed`). Two tools read it as one job: `plan_run.py`, which
plans one bench per point, takes the time of all the repeats as that
bench's estimate, and `audit_activity.py` lists the aggregate, with the
sums, beside the per-process records.

## Unwritten outputs

`torch.empty` hands a call the block that an earlier call just freed, with
what that call wrote: for the checks, the outputs of the previous
implementation of the same repro, which have the same sizes and are
correct. A kernel that skips part of its output then returns the right
values there. A variant of pointwise_732ce9dc68ae's port that never writes
one of its nine window slots passed the anchored gate with an error of 0.0
when the gate had judged the unedited port just before. In `bench.py` a
call is judged as a replay of a CUDA graph, whose outputs come from a pool
of the capture's own in fresh device memory; that read as zeros, so there
an unwritten element passed wherever its right value is zero.

Every call the checks judge therefore starts from poisoned memory
(`common.POISON_BYTE`, 0xFF: -1 in the signed integers and a NaN in the
float dtypes, except the two fp8 "fnuz" formats, where it is a finite
value and only the byte comparison below tells an unwritten element):

- `common.poison_free_memory` fills every block that PyTorch's allocator
  holds free, and those whose free waits for another stream's work
  (`Tensor.record_stream`), which the next allocation can hand out. It
  fills them in place, before the default compile's call that makes the
  gate's baseline, before every candidate the gate judges, and before each
  call that `check_port.py` and `autotune.py` check. Such a call runs
  again when the allocator took fresh memory from the driver during it,
  which the fill did not reach, and one that takes fresh memory in each
  of three calls fails as unjudged (`UnfilledMemory`): fresh memory reads
  as zeros, which would pass for an unwritten zero.
- `bench.py` fills the outputs that a captured call allocated, and the
  buffers it freed, between the capture and the replay it judges.
- `common.check_written` then looks at the outputs' bytes: one that holds
  an element of the poison byte alone is run once more on memory filled
  with 0x00, and an element that is the first byte in the first run and
  the second in the second was never written. That fails the
  implementation (`UnwrittenOutput`; in `bench.py` it is not timed)
  whatever the right value is, also where it is 0, -1 or `True` and for
  outputs that no reference fixes.

One case is out of its reach: an output allocated in the block of a buffer
that the same call freed earlier starts from what the call itself wrote
there, not from the fill. An element left unwritten in it shows only as a
wrong value, and not at all if the freed buffer held the output's values.

All 150 ports passed this on both backends at their default configs when
the checks were added, and so did the CuTe ports pinned to a campaign's
picks (the two U-Net ports, pointwise_457ec7dce9c1 and
pointwise_87cb1a76ed86, to the picks of a search of their concatenation
kernel alone). `check_port.py --configs-dir RUN/configs` repeats it for a
run's picks.

## Other Triton builds: fbtriton and Tile IR

The Inductor side of a bench is whatever the Triton of the bench's
environment compiles Inductor's kernels to. A baseline is the same benches
in another environment, with the same Inductor and another Triton build or
another path through one:

- fbtriton (https://github.com/facebookexperimental/triton) with
  `TRITON_USE_META_WS=1`, the switch of Meta's warp specialization. It
  specializes no kernel of the study's 150 points: Inductor marks a loop
  for it only in its matmul templates, and a compile census of the points'
  Inductor kernels (529 kernel configs, the switch off and on) finds no
  warp-specialized loop in either state. There the switch changes the
  code in substance only for the mix-order reductions of 11 points (ten
  sum_sum_sum points and sum_sum_2cd99e5eb23e), whose pipelined loop gets
  a peeled epilogue (1.6 to 2.6 times the instructions); of the other
  compiles 391 are identical, 125 hold the same instructions in another
  order and 2 differ in a few instructions. What the baseline shows
  elsewhere is fbtriton's own code generation, which differs from stock
  Triton's in every compile of a sample (192 compiles of 51 points);
- the Triton-to-tile-IR fork (https://github.com/triton-lang/Triton-to-tile-IR)
  with `ENABLE_TILE=1`, its CUDA Tile IR backend. "Tile IR" in the study
  is the fork's main (1dd8e2f2: Triton 3.9.0 base, CUDA Tile IR 13.4), not
  the older pin of Helion's CI (0d9283bf: Triton 3.6 base). Under either,
  Inductor's kernels compile only with
  `TORCHINDUCTOR_USE_STATIC_CUDA_LAUNCHER=0` (A baseline run, below), and
  one of the 150 points has no Tile IR kernel: the SELU kernel of
  pointwise_b6d762cd3d81 calls `libdevice.expm1`, which the backend does
  not lower.

Both switches are environment variables that the wrong Triton ignores, and
Inductor's FX-graph cache does not key on either, so nothing in a timing
says which compiler made it. The harness therefore records the build and
refuses to run where the switch cannot hold:

- Every bench record names its Triton (`triton_backend`,
  `common.triton_backend`): the distribution that provides the `triton`
  package (`triton`, `fbtriton`, the fork's `triton` or `triton_tileir`;
  where several claim the package, one installed over another, the one
  whose record of `libtriton.so` is the installed file's) with its version
  and commit, the values of the two switches and what the build makes of
  them (`meta_ws`, `tile`), the other `TRITON_*`, `TILEIR_*` and
  `TORCHINDUCTOR_*` variables that are set, whether Inductor launches the
  kernels itself (`static_cuda_launcher`), and a name: `stock`, `fbtriton`,
  `fbtriton+meta_ws`, `tileir`, or `tileir-fork-ptx` for the fork with its
  switch off. `versions.triton` is that distribution's version (it was
  None under fbtriton, whose distribution is not named triton).
- A bench started with a switch that cannot be in effect ends before it
  compiles anything, with an error record that says why
  (`bench.preflight`, `common.check_triton_switches`): `TRITON_USE_META_WS`
  under a Triton without the knob (`knobs.nvidia.use_meta_ws`: anything but
  fbtriton), `ENABLE_TILE` or `TRITON_DEFAULT_BACKEND=tileir` under a
  Triton without a Tile IR backend, or a value the build does not read as
  on (the fork reads `ENABLE_TILE=1` only). And with Tile IR in effect, by
  `ENABLE_TILE=1` or by `TRITON_DEFAULT_BACKEND=tileir`, which the fork's
  main reads first: a missing `tileiras`, or
  `TRITON_TILEIR_RUNTIME_FALLBACK=1`, which reruns a kernel through PTX
  when its Tile IR launch raises.
- Cache directories are one build's and one switch state's
  (`common.claim_caches`). Triton keys its kernel cache on
  `TRITON_USE_META_WS` and on the backend, but a run with the switch on
  that finds the Inductor cache of a run with it off compiles nothing and
  launches that run's cubins (measured with fbtriton). A bench with a
  switch in effect takes only directories (`TORCHINDUCTOR_CACHE_DIR`,
  `TRITON_CACHE_DIR`) that hold no kernels (the launcher modules that a
  Triton build keeps in its cache, which the fork builds as it is
  imported, are none) or that benches of the same build, switches and
  variables filled, and leaves a stamp there (`bb_triton_backend.json`);
  any bench refuses a directory with another stamp. `campaign.py` gives
  every job empty directories of its own for Triton, Inductor, Helion, the
  CuTe DSL and the CUDA compute cache (`campaign.job_env`), whatever the
  environment names, and passes the switches through; the record lists
  the two it used (`cache_directories`).
- The kernels a compile launches are checked once they exist
  (`common.artifact_issue`): each launcher's entry in `inductor_configs`
  carries what Triton's cache holds of the binary it launches (`artifact`:
  the backend it was compiled for, `cuda` or `tileir`; the Triton version
  that compiled it; the `TRITON_USE_META_WS` it was compiled under, which
  Triton writes into a kernel's metadata; the kinds of files beside it),
  and each kernel's entry the Triton build and backend that Inductor
  generated it for (`backend_hash`, which Inductor writes into a kernel's
  source: a kernel from its FX-graph cache names the run that filled it).
  A binary of another Triton, of the PTX path under `ENABLE_TILE=1`, or
  without the warp-specialization variable under `TRITON_USE_META_WS=1` is
  an error of that Inductor compile, not a timing. So is the reverse, in
  a run without a switch (stock Triton, or a baseline's control: fbtriton
  or the fork with the switch off): a binary compiled through Tile IR or
  under `TRITON_USE_META_WS`. The Triton version alone does not tell the
  builds apart, since the fork's main and stock Triton both call
  themselves 3.9.0 and a kernel's metadata names nothing else; the
  kernel's `backend_hash` against the process's own
  (`common.inductor_backend_hash`: Triton's key over its installed files,
  and the backend's) does. Where the refused compile is the default one,
  the numerics gate of the process has no anchor, as where the default
  compile fails: no implementation is judged by the other build's kernel
  or timed. The default compile is held to this before the gate is first
  anchored on it, also where a bench's listing leaves `inductor` out or
  has another implementation before it.
- A run directory holds the benches of one build and switch state
  (`<run>/triton_backend.json`, written by the first campaign that benches
  into it): a campaign started with another one, a restart from a shell
  without the baseline's environment say, stops before it changes anything.
  A directory without that file is judged by its bench records: one that
  names its build (`triton_backend`: a bench by hand, or a run whose file
  went) must be of the campaign's build and switch state; one that names
  none was made before records did and is taken for stock Triton's, so
  only a campaign with stock Triton goes on beside it, not fbtriton or
  the fork, with the switch or without.
- These are the guards of `bench.py` and `campaign.py`. The other scripts
  that compile Inductor's kernels (`check_port.py`, `autotune.py`,
  `ncu_profile.py`, `inductor_code.py`) have none: under another Triton
  build or switch, give them cache directories of that build and switch
  state alone.

### A baseline run

A baseline run benches the picks of a finished (or running) main run in the
baseline's environment, with nothing autotuned:

```bash
source BASELINE-env.sh   # the environment's python first on PATH, the switch
S=benchmarks/cute/better_benchmark
scripts/gpu_board.py run --gpus $G --purpose "better-benchmark BASELINE" -- \
    python $S/campaign.py --selection $S/selection.json --run-dir RUN-BASELINE \
    --backends cute --configs-from RUN/configs --phases bench --bench-repeats 3 \
    --bench-impls inductor,inductor_cd,inductor_max,oracle,helion_cute,memcopy \
    --gpus $G
```

The listing is the main run's, oracle included (the default of
`--configs-from` with `--backends cute`), so that every implementation is
timed in the slot and between the neighbours it has in the main run's
rounds: only the oracle and the memory copy run right behind Helion+CuTe
there, never an Inductor compile.

The campaign starts its jobs with the interpreter it was started with, from
the Helion checkout that holds `campaign.py`; use the tree that autotuned
RUN's picks, so that they generate the code that was measured (each
record's `code_matches_autotune`). The environment needs:

- a Python the Triton build has a wheel for, and a PyTorch whose Inductor is
  the main run's (compare `torch/_inductor` of the two; the records name
  `versions.torch_git`), so that only the Triton compiler differs;
- the Triton build, and for Tile IR its assembler (`tileiras`, found
  through `TRITON_TILEIRAS_PATH` or bundled with the fork) and
  `TORCHINDUCTOR_USE_STATIC_CUDA_LAUNCHER=0`: with Inductor's own launcher
  every compile fails under `ENABLE_TILE=1` (`'KernelMetadata' object has
  no attribute 'shared'`), on the fork's main as at the pin of Helion's
  CI, so the kernels are launched through the fork's launcher;
- Helion's dependencies (those of `pyproject.toml`) and, for Helion+CuTe
  in the same process, `nvidia-cutlass-dsl` at the main run's version
  installed by the steps of `scripts/install_cute.sh`. Helion itself is not
  installed;
- the better-benchmark checkout (`BETTER_BENCHMARK_ROOT`).

Where Helion+CuTe cannot run in an environment, bench the same listing
without the port and without `--configs-from`
(`--phases bench --bench-impls inductor,inductor_cd,inductor_max,oracle,memcopy`).

A baseline's kernel that fails to compile, or fails the gate, is an error
or a `FAIL` of that Inductor compile in the record, like any other. Two
things differ from a stock run. The numerics gate anchors on the process's
own default Inductor compile, so that compile fails it only where its
output is not finite where the reference is, whatever else the baseline's
Triton made of it, and a port is judged against that compile's error. And
where the default compile does not compile (`libdevice.expm1` has no Tile
IR lowering: the SELU kernel of pointwise_b6d762cd3d81) or is refused,
nothing in that process can be gated, so the record has no Helion+CuTe
timing either.

## How to reproduce

A run needs:

- A Helion checkout at the tree under test that nothing edits while the run
  lasts: the jobs import Helion from the checkout that holds `campaign.py`
  (run from a `git worktree` when you edit the compiler meanwhile).
- A better-benchmark checkout at `selection.json`'s `better_benchmark_commit`,
  found through `BETTER_BENCHMARK_ROOT` unless it sits at `../better-benchmark`
  next to the Helion checkout (next to a worktree it usually does not).
- GPUs that you hold on the machine's GPU board (`scripts/gpu_board.py`) for
  the whole run and give the campaign by number: `--gpus 0,1,2,3` under
  `scripts/gpu_board.py run --gpus 0,1,2,3 -- ...`, as in the commands below.
  `run` claims exactly those GPUs, renews the claim for as long as the
  campaign runs and releases it when the campaign ends. The claim's `--ttl`
  (one hour unless given) does not limit the run: it is at most how long the
  claim outlives a `run` that was killed, which is a reason to leave it
  short. `run` does not wait for the GPUs: when one is busy or claimed, by
  another account or by another session of yours, or holds idle processes of
  another account, it exits with status 1 and names the GPU, and no campaign
  has started. Run it again once the GPU is free (`--shared` would start
  beside those idle processes, and is not for timing); the study's launcher
  tried once a minute.
- No other account's jobs on those GPUs. Nothing in the campaign keeps it to
  a set of GPUs: the numbers you give are that guard (the first guard
  below). `--board-gpus N` leaves the choice to the board instead: it claims
  up to N free GPUs while jobs can start, renews the claims every third of
  `--board-ttl` and hands back GPUs no job can use (a renewal extends every
  claim of the board session, so run the campaign under a
  `GPU_BOARD_SESSION` of its own). It takes any GPU that is free when it
  asks, so it is for a machine where every GPU the board calls free is one
  to time on.

The study's numbers come from B200s; every record lists its GPU and the
torch, Triton, cutlass-dsl and Helion versions it ran with. `selection.json`
holds the 48 (repro, shape) points picked by model share and family, three
extra ported repros, and the next 99 repros by model share, 150 points in
all; `select_kernels.py`'s docstring has the commands that made it from
better-benchmark's all-shapes Inductor sweep and B200 model accounting.

A run in a new directory `RUN`, reusing what still holds of earlier runs
`PRIOR` (newest first) and compared with a baseline run `BASE`:

```bash
export BETTER_BENCHMARK_ROOT=/path/to/better-benchmark GPU_BOARD_SESSION=bb-RUN
S=benchmarks/cute/better_benchmark
G=0,1,2,3  # GPUs of this account that no other account's jobs use
# Plan: every CuTe autotune, the Triton autotunes whose earlier records do not
# hold, every bench. Checking a reusable record's pinned code needs a GPU of
# your own; --no-pin-check reuses nothing.
scripts/gpu_board.py run -n 1 --purpose "plan a better-benchmark run" -- \
    python $S/plan_run.py --run-dir RUN --prior PRIOR --provisional-triton --write
# Phase 1: the CuTe autotunes and the benches, which time Helion-Triton on the
# earlier runs' configs (flagged provisional in the report).
scripts/gpu_board.py run --gpus $G --purpose "better-benchmark RUN" -- \
    python $S/campaign.py --selection $S/selection.json --run-dir RUN \
    --backends cute --gpus $G
python $S/report.py RUN --baseline BASE
# Phase 2: the Triton autotunes, each point benched again after its own.
scripts/gpu_board.py run --gpus $G --purpose "better-benchmark RUN" -- \
    python $S/campaign.py --selection $S/selection.json --run-dir RUN \
    --backends triton --gpus $G
python $S/report.py RUN --baseline BASE
```

Without earlier runs one campaign does everything (the phase-1 command
without `--backends`, then `report.py RUN`). A stopped campaign picks up
where it stopped when started again with the same arguments. Phase 2 keeps
each phase-1 bench record as `bench/<point>.json.prev`, so the two phases'
Inductor and CuTe times check each other. Where the comparison with BASE is
of two trees, give the campaigns `--seeds-from BASE` (next section).
`plan_run.py --reuse-unsampled` also reuses Triton records made before the
activity sampling existed; `report.py` counts those ports only with
`--include-unsampled`, which counts every record made before the sampling.

### Comparing two trees: the same seeds

On a large kernel the pick of a cold autotune follows the initial population
that its random seed draws. In this study (two kernels, of 2.4 and 8.6 GB)
one seed started the searches of two Helion trees from the same population
and ended them at the same level of pick, while searches from different
seeds ended 4 to 10% apart, more than most compiler changes move a kernel.
A campaign gives every autotune job a fresh seed, so a before/after
comparison of two trees with one autotune each compares the trees only
where both searched from the same seed. `--seeds-from BASE` runs the
after-campaign that way. The seed draws the population from the port's
search space, so it is the same population only where both trees build the
same space: a tree that adds or changes a tunable, a seed config or the way
the population is drawn starts from another one on the same seed, and its
before/after still holds that difference. Generation 0 of the two runs'
`search.csv` (Search logs, below) shows whether the populations are the
same.

```bash
scripts/gpu_board.py run --gpus $G --purpose "better-benchmark RUN" -- \
    python $S/campaign.py --selection $S/selection.json --run-dir RUN \
    --backends cute --seeds-from BASE --gpus $G
python $S/report.py RUN --baseline BASE
```

- Each autotune takes the seed of the autotune record that stands in
  `BASE/configs` for the same (repro, shape, backend): the
  `HELION_AUTOTUNE_RANDOM_SEED` in the record's `env`, where `autotune.py`
  records its job's `HELION_*` environment. That is the seed of the search
  the pick came from whichever attempt's record stands (the first clean
  one, or the cleanest kept after contaminated attempts) and whichever run
  made it (a record `plan_run.py` reused carries its own search's). Every
  record of the study's runs has it. The `job_start` events of
  `BASE/campaign.jsonl` are no source for it: a job that started several
  times has a seed per start there, and the events do not say whose record
  stands.
- The job searches from that seed on every start. A rerun after a
  contaminated attempt repeats it, as does a restart of a stopped campaign
  with the same arguments; without the option each start draws a fresh
  seed.
- A port BASE names no seed for searches from a fresh one: BASE has no ok
  record of it, only a provisional one (another run's pick standing in for
  an autotune that did not run), or a record whose search was given no seed
  (`autotune.py` run by hand). The `seeds_from` event in
  `RUN/campaign.jsonl` lists the seed of each autotune the campaign is to
  run and, as `fresh`, the ones left on a fresh seed, with the reason. The
  campaign stops before it changes anything when BASE names no seed for any
  selected autotune (`BASE/configs` given for `BASE`, say), and when BASE
  is `RUN` itself.
- A paired autotune's record names BASE as `seed_from`, and so does its
  `job_start` event; an autotune on a fresh seed has neither. `report.py
  --baseline BASE` says how many of the autotunes the run made searched
  from the seed of BASE's autotune of the same port, and for the others
  where their seeds came from (their own, or the run `seed_from` names). It
  counts by the seeds in the two runs' records, so two runs that both took
  their seeds from a third count as paired, and a port that BASE has
  autotuned again since does not.
- The option combines with `--only`, `--retune` and a planned run: an
  autotune the campaign does not run takes no seed (a finished one, or a
  Triton record `plan_run.py` reused), and one that replaces a provisional
  record takes BASE's like any other. It has no use with `--configs-from`,
  which autotunes nothing.

The same seed does not make two searches the same. The initial population
is drawn from the seed (over the tree's search space, above), but the
generations after it follow the timings.
Nine searches of pointwise_238e96847398 from the seed of an earlier run's
all measured that run's initial population: 108 configs whose fastest and
median readings are those of its log to 0.5%, and the same configs in the
same order in the seven that kept a search log. No two of those seven
explored the same first generation (0 to 29 of its 44 to 48 configs in
common). The nine went on for 2 to 6 generations and 256 to 443 configs,
where that run's search took 2 and 256. Four ended on that run's pick (17.2
and 17.4 us in two benches' `cold_us`) and five on five others (16.2 to
16.6 us in four benches). The same seeds take the seed's share out of a
before/after, not the rest of a search's spread.

### Search logs

Every autotune job keeps the autotuner's log of its searches
(`HELION_AUTOTUNE_LOG`) in `RUN/logs/search/<repro>__<shape>__<backend>/`,
so a campaign's searches can be analysed afterwards instead of replayed
with the same seed:

- `search.csv`: two rows per benchmarked config, `started` and its outcome,
  in the order they happened, with `run_id` (one per kernel search: a port
  with several kernels appends them all), `timestamp_s`, `config_id`,
  `generation` (0 is the initial population), `status`, `perf_ms`,
  `compile_time_s` and the config.
- `search.log`: the search's messages, which the job's log under
  `RUN/logs/autotune` holds too.
- `search.sources.csv`, where a search records them: the digests of the
  code its configs generated.

A job that runs again keeps the earlier logs like its other files: a
contaminated attempt's as `<directory>.attempt<k>`, the previous run's as
`<directory>.prev`. The files of one suffix are one start's: where the
previous run left no search logs (`--no-search-logs`) or no log of its
own, the older `.prev` ones go instead of staying beside that run's
record.

A record and its `job_start` event name the files the job wrote while it
ran (`env.HELION_AUTOTUNE_LOG` and `search_log`, as `log` for the job's
log), not where they are after the job was set aside: for
`<record>.attempt<k>` or `<record>.prev`, add that suffix to the directory
and to the job's log. The record that stands for a job as its cleanest
attempt (every attempt was contaminated, or a retry failed) is a copy of
that attempt's record, so its search logs and its log are the attempt's,
which the job's `job_contaminated` event names (`kept`).

The logs are on by default (`--no-search-logs` keeps none), because they
cost a search no time that can be measured. The autotuner spends about
50 us on a config's two rows, where a config of a small port takes 0.6 s to
compile and time. The nine cold autotunes of pointwise_238e96847398 from
one seed, on B200s, measured their initial population of 108 configs in 68
to 77 s and once 86 s with the log (seven of them) and in 70 and 72 s
without (69 s in the run the seed came from); the machine's load, from
other jobs compiling, moves that more than the log can.

The CSV takes about 1.1 KB per row, two rows per config: 0.5 MB for that
port's one search of 257 configs, and 2 to 6 MB for the search of one of
the study's large kernels. A job logs every kernel of its port. The 51 CuTe
autotunes of one of the study's campaigns searched 99,419 configs, from 256
for that port to 7,103 over the four kernels of sum_sum_sum_d2c97d17f3dc,
and 1,824 in the median job. By those counts the logs take about 4 MB for
a job, 16 MB for the largest and 0.2 GB for a 51-point CuTe campaign, whose
run directory took 28 MB without them. Each attempt keeps its own, so a job
that ran three times holds three searches' logs.

The machine is shared, so the campaign guards the timings:

- Give the campaign GPUs of this account by number while other accounts'
  jobs come and go on GPUs that look free. `--board-gpus` claims any GPU
  that is free when it asks, also one that another account uses in bursts
  without a claim on the board. A campaign of this study took such a GPU:
  the other account's jobs came back, five attempts of the autotunes it ran
  there were contaminated (one port's all three, so a contaminated record
  stood for it) and one point was benched there. Hold the GPUs on the board
  for the whole run instead and give the campaign the same ones, as the
  commands above do (`gpu_board.py run --gpus $G ... campaign.py --gpus
  $G`). This guard is the operator's: the campaign runs on the GPUs it is
  given, or on those the board hands it, and checks only what else runs on
  them (the guards below).
- A job starts only on a GPU that other accounts' processes keep below the
  5% that would contaminate it (below) and where no process of this account
  that the campaign did not start runs.
- Every job samples `nvidia-smi pmon` on its GPU once a second. A process
  outside the job that reaches 5% SM utilization (`common.CONTAMINATION_SM_PCT`)
  contaminates a bench record. An autotune record is contaminated once such
  processes have computed at that level for 1% of the job and at least 10 s
  (`common.AUTOTUNE_CONTAMINATION_SHARE`, `AUTOTUNE_CONTAMINATION_MIN_S`):
  a blip moves only a few of a long autotune's timings.
- The campaign runs a contaminated job again, and one whose activity
  samples do not cover it, up to `--retry-contaminated` times (default 2),
  on another GPU when one is free, keeping each such attempt as
  `<record>.attempt<k>` and its log as `<log>.attempt<k>`. The first clean,
  covered attempt stands; when
  every attempt is contaminated or not covered, the best does (a covered
  one first), flagged `contaminated: true`.
- `report.py` leaves contaminated records, and records whose activity
  samples do not cover their jobs (`common.activity_coverage_issue`), out of
  every aggregate (a bench record its kernel, an autotune record its port)
  and lists them. A port's timing counts only while its
  point's autotune record holds the kernels the bench pinned, so a phase-1
  timing on a provisional config stays out until the point is benched again.
